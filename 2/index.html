The top row of images shows the unaligned result — the raw R, G, and B plates stacked directly on top of each other with no correction. The bottom row shows the result after aligning with a brute-force window search: for every image, I checked every possible integer shift in the range -15 to 15 for both the vertical and horizontal offset, scored each candidate, and kept the best one.

One early issue I ran into was with cropping. Without it, edge pixels — plate borders, scan noise, and other artifacts along the image boundary — were being factored into the alignment score, even though they have nothing to do with how well the actual photo content lines up. Since those edges can be cropped differently or contain artifacts that shouldn't influence the "true" alignment, I added a border crop before scoring so the search only considers the meaningful interior of the image.

I also tested both NCC and L2 as scoring metrics and found they landed on the same offsets for the smaller jpeg images, and a maximum of a difference of 2 in each direction for the larger tif images. L2 and NCC are two different ways of scoring how well a candidate alignment matches. L2 (sum of squared differences) directly subtracts pixel values and squares the result — it measures the raw magnitude of difference between two images, with no normalization involved. NCC (normalized cross-correlation), first subtracts each region's own mean and divides by its own magnitude before comparing, meaning it measures whether the pattern of light and dark matches, independent of each channel's absolute brightness or contrast level. Because of this, L2 is a distance (lower is better, aiming to minimize), while NCC is a similarity score ranging from -1 to 1 (higher is better, aiming to maximize) — the two require opposite comparison logic when searching for the best offset.

Since NCC subtracts each region's mean and normalizes by its magnitude before comparing, this makes it more accurate to identifying similarity for the presence of objects when there are brightness/contrast differences between channels. This is different from the L2 formula, because there is normalization in NCC as opposed to none in L2. The L2 formula instead just finds the difference between the two images. However, when the channels being compared don't actually have much of a brightness/contrast difference to begin with, the normalization only rescales the score values, and won’t really change which alignment is the best. Thus, for images where the R/G/B intensities were already reasonably consistent, NCC and L2 converged on similar if not the same answer.

Searching over a single fixed window becomes prohibitively expensive as images get larger. For an image like melons.tif, finding the optimal offset could require searching over a window of 200+ pixels in each direction.

Therefore, I looked into using image pyramids. Image pyramids solve searching through hundreds of shifts for large images by rescaling the image down to a small base size (I used a cutoff of 500 pixels along the shorter dimension) and searching there first. Because the image has been scaled down so much, a wide window suddenly takes much less time since each pixel in the small image pyramid essentially represents a larger group of pixels in the original image.

I implemented this recursively: at each level, the function calls itself on a half-resolution version of the image, recurses all the way down to the base case, and then performs the same -15 to 15 window search used in the single-pixel approach to get the starting point.

Once that base-case search finds its best match, the recursion unwinds back up through the pyramid. At each level going back up, the image is scaled back up by a factor of 2 (matching the 0.5x downscaling done by sk.transform.rescale), and the shift found at the level below is scaled by that same factor of 2. This is because for every one pixel of shift in the reduced image, it is equivalent to 2 pixels in the following image pyramid, and so on.

This new shift then serves as the starting point for the next search, since we’ve narrowed the window of area that we had to search through by using the smaller image pyramid to compare similarities. This means each level only has to search a small window around the estimate, as opposed to starting from scratch. For most of the .tif images in this set, the recursion went four levels deep. That means the total work done is roughly four rounds of a 15×15 window search, instead of one enormous 400×400 window search at full resolution, which is a significant reduction in the number of shifted images compared to each other.

I tested both NCC and L2 in the multi-scale pyramid search, since the pyramid only changes what gets searched (a small window at each resolution level, refined and scaled up level by level) rather than how each candidate is scored. At every level of the pyramid — from the coarsest base case up to full resolution — I used the same L2/NCC scoring functions to evaluate each candidate offset within that level's local search window, and combined that level's winning offset with the scaled-up estimate carried from the level below. This meant the pyramid version got the same robustness benefits from NCC as the single-pixel version, while also being far faster, since the wide search only ever happened once at the smallest, coarsest image rather than at full resolution.

The Emir was the only image that didn't align correctly. This is because when aligning the red and green matrices against the blue matrix, the deeply saturated color of the robe shows up as almost white in the blue channel, but somewhat dark in the green channel and extremely dark in the red channel. Therefore, there were specifically issues with aligning the blue and red matrices since the difference in the robe color threw off the NCC and L2 calculations.

Therefore, I instead tried aligning the images using the green matrix, since the difference in colors between the blue and red were much larger than the difference of the blue and green and green and red. This was much more successful, and the Emir was successfully aligned.
